# 8. A second text: 「酒虫」

**Question.** Every change so far was tuned on one short story, so we may be overfitting to it. Does the house hold up on a different text? This was also the first run of three new pieces of machinery:
- the emergency lever;
- the up-front cost estimate;
- the desk retry.

**The text.** 「酒虫」 (The Wine Worm), by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (1916), retells a Chinese tale. A rich drinker has a "wine worm" removed by a foreign monk, and then loses his health and fortune. It's 6,700 characters, and the house splits it into two chapters. The narrator's voice is wry and mock-scholarly, and the closing section offers three interpretations with a straight face. It's a very different text from 「晩餐」.

**Set-up.** The current house: the plain drafting prompt, Spark readers allowed to propose rewrites, and a Wonder desk that sees the translator's notes. It's compared blind with the one-shot and with Glenn W. Shaw's translation in *Tales Grotesque and Curious* (1930). Shaw is a professional human translator who works close to the Japanese. That makes his version one useful comparison, not a gold standard.

## How the run went

- **The estimate held.** It priced the run at $2.14 and the run cost $1.11. The estimate is meant to come out high, so that a run that starts doesn't run out of money half-way.
- **Every note was decided.** The readers raised 39 notes across the two chapters. The desk took 32, declined 5, and sent 2 up to the editor, both of them real questions: whether to gloss 竹婦人 ("bamboo wife") and the measure 寸. No edit was refused and no note went unaddressed.
- **The lever ran end to end.** After the last chapter, the house took the first chapter back to the desk with the finding from the second. But the finding was a false alarm: the desk had written an entry that amounted to "nothing here changes an earlier chapter". The desk declined it correctly on the way back, at the cost of one extra call. The schema now says that "nothing" is an empty list.

## The blind audit

| | Fidelity (critical / serious / minor) | Translationese (serious / minor) | Rank |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The house** | 0 / 1 / 5 | 2 / 11 | 1 |
| Shaw (1930) | 0 / 2 / 14 | 1 / 7 | 2 |
| One-shot | 0 / 1 / 11 | 11 / 14 | 3 |

- **The house ranked first,** as "the most faithful, with the best-kept ironic register and the cleanest closing". It keeps 蛮僧 as "barbarian monk", the 畝 as mu, and the narrator's "rather" in the closing three answers ("should rather be called happiness for Liu"). Those small words are where the irony lives.
- **Shaw is the easiest read,** and it has a real English voice. But it mishandles terms across the whole story: a "mountain priest" where the monk is a foreigner, "three hundred acres" for three hundred mu (about six times too much land), and checkers for go.
- **The one-shot did badly on this text.** It had 11 serious stiff sentences, against 2 for the house, and on 「晩餐」 the gap was much smaller. The house's value shows more on harder prose.
- **The house's one serious slip was ours.** It used Japanese readings for Chinese names and places ("Sun Sensei", "Chozan", "Hodoji", "three sun") alongside pinyin for the people. The style sheet chose "Sun Sensei" by applying the analyst prompt's own example, which says to keep "Sensei" where the relationship lives in the title, to a Chinese scholar. The example was taken as a rule, in the wrong world.

## What changed

- **The desk schema now says that "nothing found" is an empty list.**
- **Next:** the analyst prompt's example should not decide a story set in China. The principle (keep a form of address when the relationship lives in it) needs one line saying that names and titles follow the world of the story.
