# 1. The first pipeline, and the referent map

**Question.** Does the first design make good English? In that design, a critic checked the draft against the source and the translator answered each query. And does a sentence-by-sentence referent map (who speaks, who acts, in every sentence) help the drafter?

**Set-up.** The first full run on 「晩餐」. The bible held a cast, a ledger and a referent map. Pages were drafted with and without the referent entries and compared side by side.

## What happened

- **The critic raised 18 queries and the translator accepted 17.** Every query pulled the English towards the Japanese, and nothing pulled it towards good English, so the result was faithful and stiff.
- **The referent map added nothing.** The same pages read the same with and without it. On clear sentences it repeated what the drafter could already see, since the whole story is in every call.
- **The map carried a mistake into every call.** In p7 the father hands the bowl to the mother, not the baby. The first read got this wrong, the mistake went into the cast and the map, the draft followed it, and the critic enforced it. A claim about one line, made at bible time, travelled through the whole house as if it were settled.

## What changed

- **Two readers and a desk, instead of a critic and an answer loop.** A faithful reader and a readable reader read each chapter independently. The desk is the only one who changes the text, and it gives a reason for every note it declines. There are no rounds and no re-checks (`STAGES.md`, "How the readers and the desk work").
- **No referent map.** The cast describes people, not lines. Who speaks in a given sentence is worked out by whoever reads that sentence, with the text in front of them.
- **The bible is evidence, not law.** Every prompt is told the bible is the house's best understanding before drafting, that it can be wrong, and that the passage wins.
