# Fortleben — the site, page by page

Written 1 Nov 2026. What a visitor finds at each address, what they can do there, and what's live at submission versus later. This is the contract for the site lane; `CONCEPT.md` says why, `STAGES.md` says how the text got there.

Two rules run through every page:
- **Nothing hinges on a human.** Every text is useful as machine work. Nothing unfinished is published: every edition on the site has been through the house's readers and desk, and says so once, at the top. A paragraph carries a mark only when an editor has signed it off: *editor-approved*, in words. At submission only the showcase will have any. The site says so plainly rather than implying more.
- **No dollars on the site.** Effort shows in drops of ink (`RUNNING-A-TEXT.md` §1). API prices and the house's recipes will keep changing, and a dollar figure on a page goes stale; the dollar figures live in the README and the evals, for developers and judges.

## Comments, reactions, proposals

Three different things, kept apart because they measure different things:

- **Reactions** are quick and anonymous: *beautiful · confusing · sounds off · made me stop* for how it reads, and *I think this is wrong* (which asks for a reason) for whether it's right. They can be left on a paragraph, on a note, or on an alternative in the margin debate (so readers can say they'd have preferred the other reading). Shown as small chips a reader can hide. Never as rankings, and **a reaction changes nothing by itself**: forty readers preferring an alternative doesn't swap it in. The editor sees the heatmap and the counts, and can turn a well-liked alternative into a fork to rule on. The house doesn't answer reactions; a reader who wants an answer leaves a comment or a proposal.
- **Comments** are marginalia: per paragraph, written in the right-hand margin beside it on wide screens and under it on phones, flat (no replies), with an optional name and no account. They're kept apart from the translation's own notes and never change the text.
- **Proposals** do, eventually: select a span, suggest a replacement, pick a reason (accuracy, flow, note). Once screened, it becomes a note to the desk and comes back *taken*, *declined* or *forked* with a reason, visible in the margin with the proposer's name if they gave one. Answering costs a model call, so proposals are capped per day and queue when the cap is hit.

**What moderation means here.** No human gate. Free checks in code come first: a low rate limit per cookie and per IP, a daily cap across the site, a length limit and a cap on links. A comment that passes them is screened in the background by Spark (the `reader-proposal` prompt), the name included, for spam, abuse, personal data and anything unfit to publish, and for being plainly unrelated to the book. Until it passes, only its writer sees it; if it fails, they see why and can reword. Every published comment has a *report* link (reports have their own rate limit per address, a little higher than comments'); reports from two different addresses hide it at once. Nothing is deleted: a failed or hidden comment stays in the database with its reason, and simply disappears from the page. Once a day the editor gets one email (through Resend) listing the day's comments, what the screen stopped and what readers hid, each with a signed link to hide or publish it; the link opens a page that asks, and only its button acts. `keikaku comments` lists, hides and restores from the command line. A takedown contact is the last backstop. This keeps to the house rule that nothing hinges on a person being available, and it means the site behaves the same during judging week whether or not anyone is watching it.

## How it grows

Written for the page, so it has to be honest:

- **Requests and votes decide what's next; an editor decides what runs.** No self-serve translation for now: the rights check, the cost and the moderation all need a person in the loop, and there's one. A text with no editor still runs, and its edition says the reading and voice weren't reviewed.
- **The wanted list is a public signal.** Which untranslated books readers want, including in-copyright ones we'll never touch, is useful to translators and presses. We'd rather a person translated them.
- **Funding a book.** The plan, not yet built: a book on the wanted list can carry a target; once readers have contributed that much, it's run through the house and published for everyone, free, as every edition here is. Nobody buys a translation; they fund one for everyone.
- **Supporting the house.** Also a plan: EPUB editions sold at a modest price on the usual stores, as a way to give something back. Everything in them is free here and anyone can generate their own, which the listing will say.
- **Other languages.** Japanese is first because it's the editor's own reading. The house is built per source language: a text comes in through a source adapter (Aozora is one), the story bible and the departments are the same, and what's specific to a language (furigana and ruby for Japanese, say) is added as that language arrives.
- **What stays the same as it grows:** every line labelled, nothing ranked by popularity, no human-sounding personas, public-domain sources only, never "human-quality".
