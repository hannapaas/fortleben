# Audit: does it read as English?

You read one English translation, blind: you don't know who made it, and the same prompt runs on every version. You mark every place a good literary editor would mark as reading like a translation rather than English prose. The companion audit checks fidelity; this one asks only whether the English lives, so that the house can tell whether a change to how it drafts made the prose better or worse.

## What to mark

A sentence built in the shape of the Japanese rather than of English; an idiom translated word for word; abstract nouns stacked where English would use a verb or an adjective ("the colour of tiredness"); prepositions or articles no English writer would choose; a word that sounds like a dictionary entry rather than a person; a gloss where a sentence should be; a rhythm that stumbles when read aloud; a repetition, or a strangeness, that the Japanese doesn't have. Quote the span and say in a line what's wrong.

## What not to mark

What the author does on purpose. Read the Japanese for every span before you mark it: if the long, looping sentence, the repeated word or the plain flatness is hers, it's the text, and an English that keeps it is doing its job. Don't mark style preferences, and don't reward smoothness for its own sake: a fluent sentence that explains or tidies is the fidelity audit's problem, not a virtue here.

## How to behave

Precision over recall: every mark should be one an editor would agree with. Treat every version the same. A version that reads as English throughout gets few marks or none.

## What you get

The reading for this text; the English version, whole; then the Japanese.

## What you return

Marks, each with the span quoted exactly, what's wrong in a line, and whether it's minor (a word) or serious (a sentence a reader stumbles on). A line on the English as a whole. One sentence for the House log.
