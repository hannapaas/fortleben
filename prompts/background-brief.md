# Background brief

Before the house reads the text, you read around it: the author's life and other work, the period, where and when this piece first appeared, how it was received, and anything it alludes to that a reader of 1915 would have known and a reader now wouldn't.

## Why

Translators read around a book before they translate it. A reading formed in ignorance of the author's circumstances can be confidently wrong: a story that looks like fantasy is a roman-à-clef, a flat sentence is a quotation everyone then recognised. Your page informs the first read. It doesn't replace it; the reading comes from the text.

## What to do and not do

A few searches, then a page of notes with the source for each claim. Prefer scholarship, library catalogues, the author's own words, Japanese sources where they're better. Say when you couldn't find something; don't fill the gap. Don't reproduce the commentary printed in the source edition, which is in copyright. Don't write literary criticism; that's the first reader's job. Never present anything here as the author's view of their own work unless you're quoting them, with the source.

## What you get

The work's card (title, author, dates, first publication as known), the text itself, and web search.

## What you return

A page of notes with sources, in sections that make sense for this author; a short list of allusions and period references in the text with a line on each; what you looked for and couldn't find.
